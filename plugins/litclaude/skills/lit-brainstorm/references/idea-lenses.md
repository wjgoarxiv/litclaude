# Idea lenses

A lens is a deliberate angle for generating options. One lens produces one family of ideas, so a
brainstorm that uses a single lens produces a single family. The diverging step of `lit-brainstorm`
uses at least four lenses and names the lens beside each option in the session notes.

Use each lens as a prompt for yourself (or for an idea lane): ask the question, write the options
the question surfaces, and keep going until the lens stops producing a new direction. Do not judge
while a lens is running.

## How to pick lenses

- Start with the lenses that fit the kind of ask (table below), then add one lens from a different
  family so the list cannot be all one kind of answer.
- When a lens stalls after three or four options, switch lenses instead of squeezing it.
- When running idea lanes, give each lane a different pair of lenses.
- Record the lens next to the handle: `Offbeat (inversion)`, `Kairos (borrowed)`.

| Kind of ask | Lead lenses | Add for contrast |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name or title | Naming families, analogy, inversion | Subtraction, extreme user |
| Feature or product idea | Extreme user, constraint removal, combination | Analogy, time shift |
| Technical direction | Resource swap, subtraction, inversion | Analogy, worst-idea flip |
| Process or workflow change | Time shift, subtraction, resource swap | Extreme user, combination |
| Strategy or positioning | Inversion, analogy, constraint removal | Worst-idea flip, extreme user |
| Stuck on a problem | Reframe the question, worst-idea flip, constraint removal | Analogy, combination |

## The lenses

### 1. Inversion

Ask what the opposite would look like, then ask what would make the opposite right.

- Prompt: "What if we aimed for the reverse of the goal? What would a product that actively
  discourages this behaviour look like, and which part of it is worth keeping?"
- Example (feature ideas for a habit tracker): a tracker that rewards rest days instead of streaks
  gives "rest-first scheduling" as an option.
- Example (naming): the opposite of a loud name is a plain, nearly invisible one.
- Watch for: inversions that are just negations. Keep only those that suggest a usable direction.

### 2. Constraint removal

Delete one real constraint and see what becomes possible, then ask whether a cheaper version of that
possibility survives with the constraint back.

- Prompt: "If budget, time, team size, platform or a rule were gone, what would we build? What is the
  smallest piece of that which the real constraints still allow?"
- Example: with no mobile app allowed, a text-message interface is the option the constraint hid.
- Watch for: removing every constraint at once. Remove one at a time.

### 3. Analogy from another field

Look at how an unrelated field already solved the same shape of problem and borrow the mechanism.

- Prompt: "Where has this shape of problem been solved before: logistics, medicine, games, farming,
  libraries, aviation, theatre? What did they do that we have not tried?"
- Example: onboarding as a hospital triage (route the user by urgency) or as a game tutorial
  (teach by doing the first level).
- Watch for: analogies that borrow vocabulary only. Keep the ones that bring a mechanism.

### 4. Extreme user

Design for the user at the edge, then see what generalises.

- Prompt: "Who would use this ten times as often, who would use it with one hand, who could not read
  the screen, who has no connection, who is hostile to it?"
- Example: for someone using it with wet hands on a factory line, voice and one-tap logging become
  options.
- Watch for: edge cases that only serve the edge. Keep those that also help the middle.

### 5. Subtraction

Remove parts until the idea breaks, and write down the smallest version that still works.

- Prompt: "What is the least we could ship that still answers the question? What if there were no
  settings, no accounts, no dashboard, no second screen?"
- Example: a naming shortlist of one-word names, or a feature that is one button and a number.
- Watch for: subtraction that cuts the reason for the idea. The smallest version must still do the
  one job.

### 6. Combination

Pair two unrelated options, or two parts of different options, and see whether the pair is better
than either.

- Prompt: "Take two items from the list and fuse them. What does a plain name plus a metaphor give?
  What does the cheap option plus the ambitious option give?"
- Example: pairing "local-first storage" with "shareable summary" gives a private-by-default app
  with an export card.
- Watch for: fusing everything into one bloated option. A good combination has one clear join.

### 7. Time shift

Move the question in time: far future, far past, or a deadline moved ten times closer.

- Prompt: "What would we do if it had to ship tomorrow? What would we regret in two years? What would
  a version built ten years ago have done?"
- Example: a "ship tomorrow" version is the smallest viable option; a "two years on" lens surfaces
  maintenance cost as a criterion.
- Watch for: far-future ideas that rest on technology that does not exist. Tag those `hypothesis`.

### 8. Resource swap

Replace the main resource: swap the tool, the team, the data source, the channel or the money.

- Prompt: "What if we bought instead of built, used a person instead of code, used a library instead
  of writing one, borrowed the audience of someone else?"
- Example: replacing a custom pipeline with a spreadsheet and a weekly manual check as the first
  version.
- Watch for: swaps that move the cost without removing it. Note who now pays.

### 9. Worst-idea flip

Generate the worst, most embarrassing, most harmful options on purpose, then flip each one.

- Prompt: "What is the worst name, the most annoying feature, the surest way to fail? What is the
  useful opposite of each?"
- Example: "make signup ten steps long" flips to "make signup zero steps long and ask later".
- Watch for: staying at the worst-idea list. The flip is the output; the worst idea is scaffolding.

### 10. Reframe the question

Change the question the options answer.

- Prompt: "Is this the right question? What is the real goal behind it? What would the question be if
  it were twice as big, or half as big, or about a different person?"
- Example: "which name" becomes "what does the name need to do in the first five seconds", which
  suggests descriptive names for a utility and evocative names for a lifestyle brand.
- Watch for: reframes that abandon the ask. Always return to the original question after reframing.

### 11. Existing assets

Start from what is already there: files, code, contacts, content, habits, a half-built thing.

- Prompt: "What do we already have that nobody is using? What could an existing asset do with a small
  push?"
- Example: for a repository topic, an unused module or script becomes the cheapest option.
- Watch for: sunk-cost anchoring. Include existing assets as options, not as defaults.

### 12. Audience and tone swap

Change who it is for or how it sounds and see what survives.

- Prompt: "Make it for children, for experts, for a sceptical buyer. Make it formal, playful, dry,
  warm. What changes in the options?"
- Example: the same product is named very differently for clinicians and for gamers.
- Watch for: tone that contradicts a stated constraint. Check each option against Phase 0.

## Naming families

For a naming ask, generate across families as well as lenses, so the list is not ten words of one
kind. Use at least four families.

| Family | What it is | Example shape | Typical risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Descriptive | Says what it does. | `ShiftHabit`, `TaskList` | Generic, hard to own. |
| Evocative | Suggests a feeling or image. | `Lantern`, `Tidepool` | Common, may mislead. |
| Compound | Two words joined. | `NightOwlList` | Long, may look like a mashup. |
| Borrowed | A word from another language or field. | `Kairos` | Wrong connotation in another language. |
| Invented | A coined word. | A new short word with the right sound. | Needs explaining, hard to search. |
| Metaphor | A concrete thing for an abstract idea. | `Anchor`, `Compass` | Overused. |
| Acronym | Initials that spell or read well. | Four letters from the purpose. | Forgettable, often taken. |
| Plain | An ordinary word used with a twist. | `Offbeat` | Search noise. |

Checks to run on every shortlisted name, and to record rather than assume:

- Say it aloud twice; check the sound for two audiences if the product spans languages.
- Read it in lower case, upper case and as a hashtag; look for unfortunate sub-words.
- For a Korean-reading audience, check the transliteration and the reading of each syllable, and
  that the Hangul spelling is unambiguous.
- Availability (domain, handle, registry, trademark) is a fact to check with a source. Write
  `availability: unchecked` until it has been, and never imply a name is free.

## Quantity guide

| Profile | Raw options | Lenses | Idea lanes |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Quick | 8 to 12 | 4 | 0 |
| Standard | 16 to 24 | 5 or 6 | up to 3 |
| Wide | 30 to 40 | 7 or more | up to 4 |

The count is a floor on variety. It is met by distinct directions, never by reworded duplicates; the
dedupe step in Phase 2 counts only distinct options.

## Anti-patterns

- Judging out loud during generation ("that one is too expensive").
- Using one lens for the whole list.
- Listing the same idea at three levels of polish.
- Leaving out the null option ("do nothing") and the cheap option ("the smallest version").
- Stating an unchecked fact (a competitor's feature, a market size, a name's availability) as
  established.
