# Background

Most apartment blocks built before 1990 in the trial district heat their flats with a shared gas boiler in the basement and cast-iron radiators sized for water at 80 °C. Replacing the boiler with an air-source heat pump looks simple on paper, but a heat pump runs efficiently only when the water it sends round the building is much cooler, around 45 to 55 °C. The question this trial set out to answer was a practical one: how much of an older building has to change before a heat pump can keep its flats warm through a cold winter at a running cost the residents accept.

Twelve buildings took part between October 2024 and March 2026. They ranged from 18 to 64 flats, and none had been insulated beyond the original single layer in the roof. Six received a heat pump with no other work (group A). Six received the same heat pump together with three low-cost measures: larger radiators in the coldest rooms, balanced pipework, and loft insulation (group B). All twelve kept their gas boiler as a backup for the first winter.

The two groups were matched on size and age rather than chosen at random. Each housing association put forward pairs of similar buildings and the trial team assigned one of each pair to group B by coin toss, which kept the groups close in flat count, floor area and orientation.

## Measurement approach

Each building had a heat meter on the heating circuit, an electricity meter on the heat pump, and temperature loggers in a sample of flats on the top, middle and ground floors. The seasonal coefficient of performance (SCOP) is the heat delivered over a winter divided by the electricity used to make it. A SCOP of 3 means three units of heat for each unit of electricity. Comfort was judged by the share of logged hours in which living rooms stayed at or above 20 °C between 07:00 and 23:00.

Three further terms recur below. The flow temperature is the temperature of the water leaving the heat pump for the radiators; the lower it can be while the flats stay warm, the higher the SCOP. Balancing means adjusting the valves so that each radiator receives its share of the flow, instead of the radiators nearest the plant room taking most of it. Backup hours are the hours in which the old gas boiler had to run because the heat pump alone could not hold the flow temperature.

# Efficiency results

## Two winters compared

Group B ran at lower flow temperatures and therefore at higher efficiency in both winters. In the first winter group A needed a median flow temperature of 61 °C to keep the coldest flats warm, against 52 °C in group B. The difference in SCOP followed directly: 2.4 for group A and 3.1 for group B. In the second winter, after group A had its radiators balanced as a no-cost fix, its SCOP rose to 2.7 while group B held at 3.2.

Backup boiler use tells the same story from the other side. Group A's boilers ran for 214 hours in the first winter, mostly on mornings below −5 °C, when the heat pump could not reach the flow temperature the radiators needed. Balancing halved that figure in the second winter. In group B the boilers ran for 31 hours and then 12, which is close to the hours lost to planned maintenance.

## Installation and operation

The heat pumps were sized on the measured heat demand of each building in the winter before the trial, not on the rating of the old boiler, which in every case was oversized by at least half. Installation took between four and seven working days per building, with the heating off for one day only. Two of the twelve units needed a return visit in the first month, both for controller settings rather than faults.

Noise was the concern residents raised most often before installation. The outdoor units sit in the yard or on a flat roof, and after the first month no building recorded more than one noise complaint per winter. Defrost cycles, in which the unit briefly reverses to clear ice from its coil, were visible as short dips in flow temperature on cold, damp mornings but did not show in the living-room logs.

# Comfort and cost

## The coldest flats

Average comfort figures hide where the problems were. In group A the ground-floor corner flats, which have two outside walls and an unheated basement below, missed the 20 °C mark on almost one in five cold mornings. Residents there were also the ones who asked most often for the gas boiler to be switched back on. In group B the larger radiators were placed in exactly these rooms, and complaints fell to the level of the warmer flats.

Top-floor flats sat between the two. Their roofs lose heat quickly, and in group A they were the second most common source of complaints; in group B the loft insulation brought them close to the middle floors.

## Running costs for residents

At the electricity and gas tariffs of the second winter, group B's heating cost per flat was 9 % below the gas-only cost of the same buildings in the winter before the trial. Group A's cost was 4 % above it. The difference between the groups is almost entirely explained by efficiency; the tariffs were the same, and the weather in the two winters was within 3 % of the long-term average.

# Resident experience

## Consent and disruption

Consent was the slowest part of the trial to arrange. Every flat that needed work inside had to agree a date, and in the first two buildings of group B the radiator work took eleven weeks to schedule for nine days of fitting. The trial team then changed its approach: a single open afternoon in the building, a printed plan showing which rooms were affected, and a choice of two dates per flat. Scheduling in the remaining four buildings took between three and five weeks.

Work in a flat took one day for a radiator swap and half a day where only valves were changed. No resident in either group asked to leave the trial, and two flats in group A asked to have their radiators enlarged after hearing about the results in group B.

## Bills and controls

Residents paid for heat through the same service charge as before, so the change of fuel did not show on their bills as a new line. Each flat kept its radiator valves, and the trial added a room thermostat in the living room of every flat in both groups. Residents who turned the thermostat above 22 °C were rare, and the logs show no rise in average room temperature after the heat pumps started, which suggests that comfort, rather than extra heat use, explains the drop in complaints.

# Discussion

The trial supports a modest conclusion. A heat pump alone can heat an older block, but it then runs hot and less efficiently, leans on the backup boiler in cold spells, and costs residents slightly more than gas. Three inexpensive measures, chosen by looking at which rooms are coldest, close most of that gap. None of the group B measures involved work inside more than a third of the flats, which mattered for consent: residents were far more willing to accept a day of work in one room than a whole-flat retrofit.

Two limits apply. The trial buildings were all of one construction type, brick with timber floors, and the results may not carry over to concrete panel blocks with different heat losses. And two winters is a short record for equipment expected to last fifteen years; the maintenance cost of the heat pumps is not yet known with any confidence.

A third question sits outside the buildings. Each heat pump raised the building's peak electricity demand by between 25 and 60 kW, and in three cases the network operator had to upgrade the supply cable before installation. A district-wide programme would need that work planned in advance, street by street, rather than building by building as in the trial.

::: callout kind=note title="What the trial did not test"
Wall insulation and window replacement were left out on purpose. Both would lower flow temperatures further, but at costs per flat that the housing associations involved could not fund within the trial period.
:::

# Recommendations

For housing associations planning similar retrofits, the trial points to a sequence. First, log temperatures for a few weeks to find the coldest rooms. Second, balance the existing pipework, which costs little and in group A alone lifted SCOP by about an eighth, from 2.4 to 2.7. Third, enlarge radiators where the logs show rooms that stay cold, and insulate the roof if it has not been done. Only then size and install the heat pump, keeping the old boiler for one winter as a safety net.

The cost of these measures was about a fifth of the heat pump installation itself, whose median was 320 thousand per building. Spent before the heat pump arrives, it lowers the size of heat pump a building needs, which recovers part of the cost at the outset.

# Further work

The meters stay in place for a third winter, which will give the first maintenance figures and a longer record for the backup boilers, now switched off in group B. A second trial in four concrete panel blocks is planned for the winter of 2027, using the same groups and measures so that the two construction types can be compared directly. The trial team will also publish its room-logging method, so that housing associations can find their coldest rooms before they commission any design work.
